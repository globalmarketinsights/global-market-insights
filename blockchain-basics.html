Blockchain explained

Learn how blockchains store shared records, group transactions into blocks and use consensus to coordinate a network.

← Back to all guides

What is a blockchain?

A blockchain is a type of shared digital record. Information is grouped into blocks, and each block is linked cryptographically to the one before it. Copies of the record may be maintained by many computers participating in the network.

The design can make past records difficult to alter unnoticed, but it does not make every blockchain application secure, accurate or trustworthy.

How a transaction moves through a network

  1. A user creates and signs a transaction using a wallet.
  2. The transaction is broadcast to the network.
  3. Participating nodes check whether it follows the network's rules.
  4. A miner or validator helps include valid transactions in a new block, depending on the network's design.
  5. Other participants verify the block and update their copy of the ledger.

What consensus means

Consensus is the method a network uses to agree on the order and validity of records. Proof of work and proof of stake are two well-known approaches. They have different security assumptions, resource requirements and incentive structures.

Public and private blockchains

Public networks are generally open for people to inspect and participate in according to the protocol. Private or permissioned networks restrict who can read, write or validate records. The word blockchain alone does not tell you how decentralised or censorship-resistant a system is.

Important limitations

  • Transactions may be difficult or impossible to reverse.
  • Network fees and confirmation times can change.
  • Smart contracts can contain bugs or design flaws.
  • Scams and misleading applications can exist on otherwise legitimate networks.
  • Some networks depend on a relatively small set of operators or validators.

Questions to ask

Before using a blockchain application, ask who operates it, how transactions are validated, what fees apply, whether code has been independently reviewed and what happens if something goes wrong.

Remember: This guide is for general education, not personal financial advice. Research independently and understand the risks before making financial decisions.
